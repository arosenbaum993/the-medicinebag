The Medicine Bag

Based on the short story by Virginia Driving Hawk Sneve

Virginia Driving Hawk Sneve is a member of the Rosebud Sioux Tribe. This version keeps the events of her story but uses shorter sentences and simpler words. It is not the full text.

Words to know

reservation
land set aside for a Native nation to live on and govern
medicine bag
a small leather pouch that holds sacred items and is passed down in a family
sacred
holy, treated with deep respect
sage
a plant used in Sioux ceremonies
blacksmith
a person who shapes iron into tools
Purpose for readingNotice what Martin does at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end of the story.
1

Martin and his little sister Cheryl lived in a city. Their great-grandfather, Joe Iron Shell, lived far away on the Rosebud Reservation in South Dakota. Every summer the family drove out to visit him.

2

Martin told his friends stories about Grandpa. He showed them the small drum Grandpa had made. He taught them the Sioux chant Grandpa had taught him. His friends were impressed.

3

Martin had one picture of Grandpa. He kept it in a drawer. He never took it out.

4

One hot afternoon, Martin heard dogs barking. He walked to the curb. An old man in a tall black hat was coming down the middle of the street. Kids and dogs followed him. Martin knew that hat.

Martin stood still for a long moment. Then the dogs snapped at the old man’s legs. Martin ran into the street and chased them off.

“Grandpa,” he said.

Grandpa set down his suitcase and shook Martin’s hand. Neighbors watched from their porches. Grandpa wore a wrinkled black suit and a bright red shirt. Martin looked down at the sidewalk. Then he looked at Grandpa’s face. Grandpa’s hand was shaking. Martin picked up the suitcase and walked him to the house.

5

Cheryl came running out the front door. “Grandpa! You came to see us!” She threw her arms around him. Martin watched her do it. Then Grandpa’s knees gave out and he fell.

6

The doctor said Grandpa was worn out from the heat. When Grandpa woke up, he explained. He had ridden a bus for two and a half days. Then he had walked, because he could not find the street. He was eighty-six years old. He had never left the reservation before.

7

The next day Grandpa spoke to the whole family. “It is soon time for Martin to have the medicine bag.”

Martin knew the bag. He had felt it on a cord around Grandpa’s neck. It was small and worn and dirty.

Martin thought about gym class. He thought about the pool. He pictured his friends looking at the bag.

Then he stood up and walked to the bed.

“Not now,” Grandpa said. “It is not time. Now I will sleep.”

8

Grandpa stayed for two months. Martin’s friends asked to meet him. Martin said Grandpa was resting. He said it again the next week, and the week after that.

Cheryl brought her friends over every afternoon. They sat on the patio and listened to Grandpa’s stories.

9

One day Martin’s friend Hank came to the door with the whole group. “We’re here to see the famous Indian of Bell View Drive.”

Grandpa was on the patio in his best clothes. He wore a beaded vest, moccasins, and the black hat with the feather.

Martin waited.

Grandpa stood up and shook every hand. “Hau, hoksilas,” he said. Boys.

Nobody laughed. The boys sat down. Grandpa talked all afternoon about hunting the deer whose skin became his vest. Martin’s mother finally sent everyone home for dinner.

“Can we come back?” Hank asked.

10

That evening Grandpa called Martin to his room. “Tomorrow it will be time.”

Martin did not sleep much.

11

The room was dim the next day. Grandpa told Martin the story of the bag.

Long ago, Grandpa’s father went alone to a hill. He fasted and prayed for three days. In a dream he saw the white man’s iron. Later he found a broken iron kettle by a stream. He put a piece of it in a bag made of elk skin.

Years later, soldiers sent him to a boarding school far from home. There he was put to work in the blacksmith shop. He spent the rest of his life working the white man’s iron.

Grandpa’s father gave him the bag. Now Grandpa held it out to Martin.

12

“Inside is the iron, a stone from the hill, and sacred sage,” Grandpa said. He opened his hand. “You do not need to wear it here. People here would not understand it. Keep it safe until you are on the reservation. Wear it then, when you put in new sage.”

Martin took the bag. “Thank you, Grandpa.”

13

That night Martin’s parents took Grandpa to the hospital.

14

Two weeks later, Martin stood alone on the prairie of the reservation. He opened the medicine bag and put fresh sage inside.
